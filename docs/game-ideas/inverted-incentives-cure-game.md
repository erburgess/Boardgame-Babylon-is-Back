# Game Idea: The Cure Problem (working title)

**Status:** Concept note for Zelazny to track
**Logged:** 2026-11-09
**Originator:** Eric Burgess

## The idea in Eric's words (condensed)

A game where the victory point system and the economy work against the players. Nobody can win unless the table collectively drives the traditional means of scoring down to zero. The model is a real problem in the US economy: a healthcare or pharmaceutical company that actually cures a disease destroys the revenue stream that disease created. The public good requires cures, yet the financial incentive rewards treatment over resolution.

Players build a conventional engine knowing from the start that the engine must fall apart by the end. A winner exists only if the shared goal is achieved, and the shared goal is the steady erosion of everyone's ability to score.

## Why it is worth pursuing

Most engine builders reward compounding. This one asks players to build a machine whose correct use is its own dismantling, which makes the mechanism the argument. Players should finish a session having felt the incentive problem in their hands rather than having read about it. That is a strong fit for a game meant to provoke a conversation about how we design incentives.

## Honest assessment and prior art

The "everyone loses unless" frame is established in semi-cooperative design (Republic of Rome, CO2: Second Chance, Archipelago, Cutthroat Caverns). The fresh territory here is the inversion of the engine itself: the shared victory condition is reached by destroying the very thing that generates individual score. That twist is uncommon and is where the design's identity lives.

The central risk is the free-rider problem. If the winner is simply whoever holds the most points when the cure condition is met, the dominant strategy is to let rivals do the curing while you keep farming treatments. The game then rewards exactly the behavior it critiques, which could be a deliberate satirical point but will feel unsatisfying if left unaddressed. A second risk is the familiar semi-coop failure where a player who knows they cannot win tanks the shared goal. Both need answers before prototyping.

## Sketch of core systems (for exploration, not commitment)

**Afflictions.** A public row of disease cards, each a market. Players place treatment products on afflictions to earn recurring revenue every round.

**Cures.** Any player can invest in research to cure an affliction. A cure removes the card from the game permanently, wiping out every treatment on it, including the curer's own.

**Public Health clock.** Uncured afflictions advance a collapse track each round. If it maxes out, the system fails and everyone loses. Curing pushes it back.

**Scoring that resists free-riding.** Candidate approaches to test:
1. *Legacy multiplier.* Final score equals banked revenue multiplied by a Legacy value earned only by funding cures. A player with large revenue and no Legacy scores near zero.
2. *Revenue converts only at the end.* Money is not points. At game end, each player converts cash to points at a rate set by how many afflictions they personally helped cure.
3. *Patent payout.* Curing grants a one-time windfall, smaller than the lifetime treatment revenue it destroys but immediate and certain, so the tension becomes timing rather than refusal.
4. *Hidden lobbying.* Players may secretly spend to slow cures, and exposure carries a heavy penalty, adding a social deduction layer that mirrors real regulatory capture.

**Endgame condition.** The game can produce a winner only when the active affliction row is empty (or below a threshold) before the clock expires. Otherwise no one wins.

## Open questions

What is the right length so the arc from "build the engine" to "tear it down" feels earned? Should the theme stay explicitly medical or abstract into a broader "solving the problem kills the business" frame (energy, security, attention economy)? Is the experience stronger fully cooperative with a single shared score, or competitive with the scoring fixes above? What does a satisfying final turn look like when the last cure lands?
